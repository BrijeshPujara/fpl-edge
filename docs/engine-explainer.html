FPL Edge · How it works

How the engine thinks

Fantasy football is a shopping problem wearing a football shirt. Here is how a machine goes about solving it — and the one thing it turned out to be badly wrong about.

Only two questions

You have £100m. You buy fifteen players. Eleven of them score each week, and there are rules about how many you can take from any one club.

That is the entire game, and it contains exactly two questions:

How many points will each player score? That is a forecasting problem. Given that, which fifteen should I own? That is a shopping problem.

Most people mash the two together and end up buying whoever did well last Saturday. Keeping them apart is the whole design.

ONE

The forecast

The engine never predicts that a player will score. It predicts expected points — the average across every way the match could go.

It is the same idea as a weather forecast. "70% chance of rain" is not a promise that it rains; it is a summary of many possible days. When the engine says a player is worth 6.2 points, it means: replay this fixture a thousand times, and he averages 6.2.

What goes into it

  1. 01

    Will he even be on the pitch?

    By a distance the biggest question, and the one the engine was originally worst at. More on that below.

  2. 02

    If he plays, how likely is he to score?

    Not "did he score last week" — that is mostly noise. It uses shot quality: how many chances he gets and how good they are. A striker who missed three sitters had a good week by this measure, because next week he gets three more.

  3. 03

    Will his team keep a clean sheet?

    Defenders and goalkeepers earn most of their points this way, so this leans heavily on who they happen to be playing.

  4. 04

    Does he take the penalties and the corners?

    A penalty is the most valuable event in football, and roughly one team in the league gets one every eight matches. Knowing who picks up the ball is worth about half a point a match — genuinely more than being a somewhat better footballer.

Then all of it is scaled back down by how likely he is to actually be out there. A brilliant player with a 40% chance of starting is worth less than a modest one who is guaranteed ninety minutes.

The thing it was wrong about

Football models are supposed to be about football. This one's largest error had nothing to do with football at all.

Across a full season of records, most rows are not a performance. They are a player who did not appear: injured, benched, rotated, forgotten. The first version of the engine assumed anyone in a squad had a fair chance of coming on for twenty minutes.

Deciding who is on the pitch turned out to matter more than everything the model knows about football, combined.

This is worth dwelling on, because it is the reason to build a thing like this at all. Nobody's intuition would have ranked "am I sure he plays" above shot quality, fixture difficulty and form put together. The season of data says otherwise, and it says so unambiguously.

TWO

The shopping

Here it stops being a spreadsheet.

The obvious move is to rank all 600 players and buy the top fifteen. That fails immediately, because you would exhaust the budget in about four signings. What you actually want is the best combination you can afford, which is a completely different question.

Think of packing a suitcase with a weight limit. You do not pack your fifteen favourite things. You pack the set that is worth the most in total while the lid still closes.

The difficulty is scale.

So the engine uses a solver: a piece of mathematics from the same family that plans airline schedules and delivery routes. It does not try every combination. It eliminates enormous regions in a single step — no squad containing this player can possibly be the best one — and closes in on what remains.

The important part is not that it is fast. It is that it does not return a good squad. It returns provably the best one, given the forecast it was handed.

Two things a spreadsheet cannot do

It tells you what money is worth today

Having chosen a squad, the engine asks a second question: if I had one more £0.1m, how many extra points could I buy with it?

It works out when to use your chips

Chips are one-shot power-ups that expire if unused. The tempting approach is a rule: use Triple Captain when someone is projected above 8.5 points.

But a rule cannot answer the only question that matters — is this week good enough, or is a better one coming before it expires? A rule fires on the first decent week and burns the chip in October, when a December fixture would have paid twice as much.

So chips are treated as a scheduling problem instead: every chip, every remaining week, one chip per week, find the best arrangement of the lot.

And it shows you the runner-up. If this week beats the next-best by a wide margin, play it with confidence. If the margin is slim, it is a coin flip and worth revisiting in seven days.

What it cannot do

It does not watch football. It has never seen a press conference, does not know a manager hinted at rotation, and cannot tell that a player looked exhausted on Saturday.

It also refuses to pretend. Early in a season, before there is enough evidence to say anything useful, it reports that it does not know rather than guessing convincingly. That is a deliberate choice and occasionally an irritating one — but a tool that manufactures confidence it has not earned is worse than no tool.
